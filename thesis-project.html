Executive Overview

VitalViz at a glance

A quick scan for busy reviewers — tap a topic for concise bullets. The full case study lives below.

  • M.Sc. thesis at McGill University (ECE), Shared Reality Lab — designing for high-stakes ICU and operating room environments.
  • Clinical vital sign monitors are essential, but their alarm-heavy, screen-fixed design creates well-documented usability failures.
  • Explored whether augmented reality could keep critical vitals perceptually available without adding more noise.
  • Built and evaluated a HoloLens 2 prototype with a 20-participant, REB-approved controlled study.
  • Alarm fatigue — clinicians face hundreds of alarms per shift; many are non-actionable, training staff to tune out the sounds that matter.
  • Monitors out of sight — during procedures, displays are often behind the clinician, forcing gaze shifts away from the patient.
  • Cognitive overload — reading waveforms and numbers under multitasking demands active interpretation (“I had to do some math to know if it was high or low”).
  • Environmental noise — auditory alarms add to ICU/OR sound levels that already exceed recommended limits.
  • Field observation and interviews with anesthesiology and HCI experts to ground design in real clinical workflows.
  • Iterative ideation — sketching notification concepts that balance salience with low distraction in busy settings.
  • High-fidelity AR prototype in Unity for HoloLens 2, iterating on animation, color, and spatial placement.
  • Controlled user study — within-subjects design with a Fruit Ninja distractor task to simulate clinical multitasking.
  • VitalViz — an AR vital sign display using three animated spheres for HR (green), BP (red), and SpO₂ (blue).
  • Visual-only alarms — urgency conveyed through size, color saturation, and motion — no additional audio noise.
  • Always in view — vitals anchored in the clinician’s field of vision via head-mounted display, regardless of position.
  • Intuitive encoding — e.g. a larger, brighter red sphere moving closer signals a high BP alarm without reading numbers.
  • ↑18% alarm detection accuracy with AR vs. a conventional monitor replica (0.98 vs. 0.80).
  • ↑15% multitasking performance — higher distractor-task scores while monitoring vitals under AR.
  • Statistically significant improvement in alarm accuracy for both non-clinicians and nursing students (p < .001).
  • No trade-off in vital trend interpretation — AR matched baseline performance on trend-reading tasks.

In Depth

I designed and developed an Augmented Reality (AR) display for vital signs, aimed at improving situational awareness in clinical settings like Intensive Care Units (ICU) and Operating Rooms (OR), where traditional bedside monitors rely heavily on auditory alarms and peripheral screens.

This system visualizes three critical vital signs: heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) — using animated, color-coded 3D spheres that are always in the clinician’s field of view via a Head-Mounted Display (HoloLens 2).

Vital Sign Monitors

what are they anyway?

Physiological parameters such as blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), and oxygen saturation (SpO₂) are critical indicators of a patient’s health condition.

Vital signs monitors continuously display these vitals and issue visual and/ or auditory alarms to alert staff about health deterioration.

Background

Alarm Fatigue & Noise Overload

ICUs and ORs are filled with constant alarms and conversations, which can overwhelm clinicians and disturb resting patients.

Limited Visibility & Accessibility

Sign monitors are often not in the clinician’s direct line of sight, causing delays and distraction due to constant shifts in focus between patient and screen.

Challenges in Critical Field Settings

In military evacuations or space-constrained scenarios, bright screens and alarm sounds can compromise safety, while bulky equipment strains limited resources.

My Solution

  • Each sphere represents a vital: HR (green), BP (red), and SpO₂ (blue).
  • Sole reliance on visuals to convey vital states (no audio noise)
  • Leveraging AR's ability to overlay virtual objects anywhere in the real world (better accessibility)
  • Vitals that always stay in clinician's field of view (lesser distraction)

Study Results

To evaluate the effectiveness of our AR display in improving alarm saliency, we conducted a two-week-long within-subjects user study comparing our AR display to a mock conventional monitor interface in a controlled lab setting.

To simulate visual workload, participants performed a distractor task using the game 'Fruit Ninja' while monitoring patient vitals.

My Learnings

The value of user feedback – Deep insights into the intricacies of their daily tasks and helped me design with real-world needs in mind.

Designing for emerging technology – Hands-on experience creating user interactions for AR applications using Unity and HoloLens.

Experiment setup matters – Attention to detail in designing the study setup is just as crucial as the application itself, to ensure valid results.

Unexpected insights from user studies – Even with a carefully crafted design, revealed surprising user requirements.
